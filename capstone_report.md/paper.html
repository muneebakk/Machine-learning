Which pages under-capture clicks? A within-client CTR screening method, checked for month-to-month persistence

Muneeba · Software Engineering student, Virtual University of Pakistan · FlyRank AI Fluency Program capstone, CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring lane

Abstract

Which already-visible pages get fewer search clicks than comparable pages from the same client, and is that gap stable or just noise? I compared each page's click-through rate (CTR) with the pooled CTR of its own client's other pages at the same average position, and flagged only shortfalls that survived an overdispersion-adjusted test. On three months of FlyRank ML Internship data (March to May 2026, 101,000 to 112,000 scored pages per month, from 47 to 56 clients with search data), pages flagged in one month earned about 0.33 times (March to April) and 0.43 times (April to May) the clicks-versus-expected of matched pages the next month. They were also 40% to 52% likely to be flagged again, against 7% to 8% for matched controls. A machine-learning model that tried to predict CTR for unseen clients did not beat a simple baseline, so the final method is a transparent benchmark, not a model. The ranking is a screening aid for human review, based on observed data, and says nothing about whether a change would recover clicks.

1. Introduction

A content team with thousands of pages cannot review them all. A useful question is which pages are visible in search results but get fewer clicks than they should. Those are the pages where a better title, meta description or a check of the results page might be worth the time. The decision this work supports is simple: which pages should a person look at first?

"Fewer clicks than they should" needs a yardstick. Raw CTR is misleading because CTR falls steeply with position, and because clients differ in brand strength, audience and topic. I therefore ask a narrower question: inside one client, which pages get fewer clicks than that client's other pages at the same position? I also ask whether the answer holds up, because a page that looks bad one month can look bad by chance.

2. Data

  • Source: the FlyRank ML Internship dataset on Hugging Face. Clients, pages and queries are anonymised hashes. No client names, domains, URLs or queries are used or shown here.

  • Final analysis: the warehouse table fact_content_daily_performance for three full months, March, April and May 2026. Only three fields were used: search impressions, search clicks and average position. Page-level values were summed or impression-weighted across each month.

3. Methodology

3.1 Two things that did not work

The pre-made flag is a rule. The example cut ships with a flag called needs_ctr_fix. A depth-3 decision tree recovered it with 99.8% accuracy from CTR, position and impressions alone (roughly: position within about 10, at least about 100 impressions, CTR at most 2%). A model trained to predict it would only rediscover that rule, so I did not use it as a target. A second flag, needs_engagement_fix, is true for every page and cannot rank anything.

A CTR model did not generalise. I trained a gradient-boosted model on position, impressions and content features and tested it on whole clients it had never seen (grouped split, so one client never appears on both sides). It barely beat a position-bucket baseline: R² 0.051 against about 0 on the plain split, and an impression-weighted R² of −0.013 against −0.022 for a Poisson version. I treat this as a negative result: position and content tiers do not predict CTR for a new client.

3.2 The method I kept: a within-client benchmark

  1. Position buckets: ≤1.5, 1.5–2.5, 2.5–3.5, 3.5–4.5, 4.5–5.5, 5.5–7.5, 7.5–10.5, 10.5–15.5, 15.5–20.5, >20.5.
  2. Expected CTR for a page: the pooled CTR (total clicks ÷ total impressions) of the other pages of the same client in the same bucket (leave-one-out), blended with the pooled CTR of all clients in that bucket. The blend weight is n / (n + K), where n is the other pages' impressions and K = 50,000.
  3. Test: clicks below expected, with the noise level widened by a dispersion factor φ. Pure luck would give φ = 1, but real pages also differ in query mix and results-page layout. φ is estimated robustly from the median squared standardised residual: 1.7 to 2.0 in the warehouse months, 4.9 in the example cut.
  4. Flag: one-sided p < 0.01 and at least 5 estimated missed clicks (expected minus actual). About 3.0% to 3.4% of scored pages are flagged each month.

A plain Poisson test flagged 10.9% of pages in the example cut. Pure noise would flag about 1%, so the overdispersion adjustment cut the flagged share to 2.4%. In that cut, changing K from 5,000 to 5,000,000 left the flagged share at 10.8% to 11.2%, and 76 to 100 of the top 100 pages stayed the same.

3.3 Validation: does the flag persist?

The flag is computed in month A. Month B is independent data, so if a gap were only luck, those pages would look normal in B. I checked two ways. First, how often flagged pages were flagged again in B. Second, a matched control comparison: for each flagged page I took non-flagged pages from the same client, the same position bucket and the same impressions tercile in A, then compared B clicks ÷ expected as a ratio of geometric means. Intervals are 95% from a client-level bootstrap (2,000 resamples), so pages from the same client are not treated as independent.

Both month pairs point the same way. One caution: matched controls are themselves flagged 7% to 8% of the time, much higher than the 2% base rate across all other pages. That is why the matched comparison matters: flagged pages tend to be larger, and larger pages are easier to flag.

4.2 The whole ranking carries signal

Pages were split into ten groups by how far below expected they were in A. The lower the group, the lower the next month's clicks ÷ expected, in both month pairs.

4.3 Where the persistent shortfall sits

1,768 pages (March to April) and 1,433 pages (April to May) were flagged in both months, from 19 and 27 clients. Most of their shortfall sits just below the top results.

4.4 What the example cut showed

On the single 30-day example cut, the top four flagged pages held about 40% of all flagged missed clicks (one page alone held 35%). Three of those four sat at position 1–1.5 and one at position 8.4, each with a CTR far below expectation, which looks more like a data or query-mix oddity than a title problem. No flagged page met the same extreme rule in the March and April warehouse windows, and I did not investigate why.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Observed, not causal. A persistent gap shows that a page keeps getting fewer clicks than its siblings. It does not show why, and it does not show that editing the page would recover clicks. I have no before-and-after change data.
  • Persistent does not mean fixable. The gap can come from query mix, branded searches, results-page features or ranking features outside the page's control.
  • Average position hides the query mix. A page's average position blends many queries. The query-level table in the warehouse (fact_content_query_90d) was not used here.
  • Missed clicks are estimates, measured against a benchmark made from other pages of the same client. If a client's whole set of pages is weak, the benchmark is weak too. It is also heavy-tailed, so a small group of pages carries a large share of the total.
  • Three months, one dataset. Two consecutive month pairs agree in direction but differ in size (0.33 versus 0.43). I have not tested seasons, other clients or other data sources.
  • The ratio has no natural reference of 1. Median clicks ÷ expected for non-flagged pages is below 1 (0.72 and 0.79), so ratios are compared between groups, not against 1.
  • Small stratum effects. Matching used client, position bucket and impressions tercile. Other differences between pages (age, topic, length) were not matched.

6. Ranked recommendations

These are suggestions for human review in priority order, based on pages flagged in both months, ranked inside each client by estimated missed clicks (top 5 per client gives 69 pages from 19 clients, or 93 from 27 clients). They are not predictions of gain.

  1. Pages at average position 3.6–10.5, flagged twice. This is the largest group: 58% to 70% of the persistent shortfall. Review the title and meta description first, since the page is already visible and the gap is stable.
  2. Pages at position 3.5 or better, flagged twice. Fewer pages, but a larger gap per page. Check the results page and query mix before changing content: branded queries or results-page features may explain a low CTR.
  3. Pages beyond position 10.5, flagged twice. 17% to 21% of the shortfall. Monitor: the main lever is ranking, not the snippet.
  4. Pages flagged only once. Monitor for one more month before spending time. Between 48% and 60% of one-month flags did not repeat.
  5. Pages with clicks under 5% of a large expectation. Verify the data and query mix first. This rule triggered four times in the example cut and not in the monthly warehouse windows.
